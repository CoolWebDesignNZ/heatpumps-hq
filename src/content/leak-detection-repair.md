## Heat pump leaking water or refrigerant?

These are two different problems with different fixes.

- **Water** comes from condensation. When the indoor unit cools or dehumidifies, moisture collects on the cold coil, drips into a tray and runs away through a drain hose. In heating mode the outdoor unit makes water instead, when it defrosts.
- **Refrigerant** is the gas sealed inside the copper pipes that carries heat between the units. It should never escape. If it does, the unit loses heating power and eventually stops.

## Heat pump leaking water inside

If water is dripping from the indoor unit or staining the wall below it, the cause is almost always in the drainage. What we look for:

- **A blocked drain.** Dust, mould slime and the occasional insect build up in the tray and the hose until water can't get past.
- **A kinked, sagging or crushed hose.** Water sits in the low point and backs up into the unit. It can happen where a hose runs inside a wall or behind a cover.
- **A unit that has dropped out of level.** The tray is designed to drain one way. If the unit tilts the other way, water spills over the front edge.
- **A failed condensate pump.** Some installs can't drain by gravity and use a small pump. When it stops, the tray overflows.
- **Ice on the indoor coil.** Very dirty filters can let the coil freeze in cooling mode. When it thaws, more water arrives than the drain can take.

The repair is usually a clear-out and flush of the tray and hose, re-routing or replacing the hose, or levelling the unit. Our [heat pump cleaning](/heat-pump-cleaning/) service deals with mould in the tray and coil.

## Heat pump dripping water from the outdoor unit

In heating mode a steady drip or a short burst of water from the outdoor unit after it has been steaming is defrost meltwater. It's what should happen. Where the water ends up is the part to get right: it shouldn't pool against the foundations, run across a path where it will freeze, or drip on a neighbour's side. A drain fitting with a hose to a gully, or raising the unit on feet over gravel, sorts that out.

## Heat pump refrigerant leak

Refrigerant leaks are usually slow. Signs to watch for:

- The unit takes longer to heat the room each week.
- Oily residue around the pipe connections at either unit. Refrigerant carries a little oil with it.
- Frost or ice on the pipe joints or valves where it doesn't normally form.
- A hissing sound near the pipes.
- The unit stopping with a fault code.

Leaks tend to start at joints, especially the flare joints where the pipes connect to the units. Pipes rubbing against a bracket or wall, and corrosion on the outdoor coil, are other causes.

## How we find and repair a refrigerant leak

1. **Pressure and temperature readings** confirm whether the charge is low.
2. **Electronic leak detection and bubble testing** around the joints, valves and coils pinpoints where it is escaping.
3. **Pressure testing with nitrogen** finds a leak that is too small to show up while the system runs.
4. **The repair**, usually re-making a joint or replacing a section of pipe. If a coil itself is leaking, we price that separately so you can weigh it against replacement.
5. **Evacuating and recharging.** The system is pulled into a vacuum to remove air and moisture, then charged by weight to the figure the manufacturer specifies.

Some newer Daikin wired controllers can detect and alert to an R32 refrigerant leak, according to [Daikin New Zealand's controller range](https://www.daikin.co.nz/pages/controllers). Most heat pumps rely on falling performance or a fault code instead.

## If you think you have a heat pump gas leak

Switch the heat pump off, open a window in the room, and keep naked flames and portable heaters away. Don't tighten the pipe nuts or try a repair kit. Over-tightened flare joints crack, and the leak gets worse.
