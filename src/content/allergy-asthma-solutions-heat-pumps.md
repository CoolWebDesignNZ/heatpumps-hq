## Why a home's heating matters for allergies and asthma

Cold, damp homes are hard on lungs. The Cardiac and Respiratory Foundation NZ says too many New Zealand homes are cold, damp and poorly ventilated, and that those conditions encourage mould, dust mites and condensation, all of which can trigger respiratory symptoms. It calls mould a well-recognised trigger for asthma.

The type of heater matters as well. The Foundation lists heat pumps as a good option for respiratory health, alongside flued gas heaters and central heating. It recommends avoiding unflued gas, kerosene and LPG heaters, which release pollutants and water vapour straight into the room, and notes that wood and pellet burners still create some air pollution.

Source: [Cardiac and Respiratory Foundation NZ, your home and your health](https://cardiacandrespiratory.org.nz/your-health/your-home-and-your-health/).

We're heat pump installers, not health professionals. For advice on asthma or allergies, talk to your doctor. What we can do is make sure the heat pump is helping rather than adding to the problem.

## How a heat pump can make allergies worse

A heat pump moves a lot of room air through its indoor unit. Over months of use:

- **Filters clog** with dust, pet hair and fibres.
- **Dust gets past** worn or poorly fitted filters and sticks to the coil and fan.
- **Mould grows** on the damp coil and in the drain tray, especially after cooling or dry mode in humid weather.
- **The fan spreads it.** Each time the unit starts, it blows air across that build-up and into the room.

Signs to look for: sneezing, itchy eyes or a cough that are worse when the heat pump is on, a musty smell at start-up, or dark specks on the louvres.

## What helps when a heat pump triggers asthma

When a heat pump triggers asthma, what helps is a clean unit inside and out, a drier house, filters suited to the job and less dust in the room.

### Clean filters

The filters behind the front panel are designed to be cleaned by you. Every few weeks while the heat pump is in heavy use is a good habit. Vacuum or rinse them, let them dry fully, and put them back. Don't run the heat pump with the filters out.

### A deep clean of the coil and fan

Filter cleaning doesn't reach the coil, the fan wheel or the drain tray, which is where mould tends to settle. A professional [heat pump clean](/heat-pump-cleaning/) treats all three. If you have allergies or asthma in the house, a yearly clean before winter makes sense.

### A dry house

A heat pump helps by keeping rooms warm, which cuts condensation on cold surfaces. Dampness still has to go somewhere. The Foundation recommends extractor fans that vent outside in the bathroom, kitchen and laundry, airing the house on fine days, and fixing leaks. A heat pump's dry mode can help in some conditions; see [using your heat pump as a dehumidifier](/heat-pump-dehumidification/).

### The right filters for the job

Standard heat pump filters protect the coil and catch the larger dust. They aren't designed to remove fine allergens the way a HEPA purifier is. Some heat pump models include extra filters or air cleaning features. If that matters to you, ask us about it when choosing a model, and we'll check the manufacturer's specification for that exact unit.

The Foundation says good filtration such as HEPA air purifiers can help reduce allergens for some households. If allergies are a real problem, a separate purifier in the bedroom alongside a clean heat pump makes more sense than expecting the heat pump to do both jobs.

### Keeping dust down

The Foundation also suggests dusting with a damp cloth and vacuuming regularly with a vacuum that has a good filter, to control dust mites.

## A heat pump in the bedroom

For a child's bedroom or yours, a small heat pump on a timer keeps the room warm overnight without adding moisture the way an unflued gas heater does. Point the louvres so air doesn't blow straight across the bed, keep the filters clean, and book a clean each year. A [floor console unit](/single-room-heat-pump-installation/) can suit rooms where a high wall unit would blow over the bed.
