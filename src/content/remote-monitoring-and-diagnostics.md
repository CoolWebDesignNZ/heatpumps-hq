## What remote monitoring means for a home heat pump

At home, it means a Wi-Fi adaptor in the indoor unit, linked to the maker's app on your phone. With it you can see:

- if the unit is on or off, and what mode it's in
- the temperature you've set, and the room temperature it's reading
- the schedules and timers you've set
- energy use, on some models and apps

It works anywhere you have internet. So you can check on the house from work or while you're away. The app depends on the brand. Each brand we fit has its own [smart heat pump](/smart-heat-pump-systems/) Wi-Fi control and app.

## What heat pump monitoring can't tell you

An app only knows what the unit's own sensors and controls know. It doesn't measure refrigerant pressure. It doesn't check the wiring, look at the drain or listen to the fan bearings. The app can show normal readings while the unit has a slow gas leak or a blocked drain.

So on a home system, remote diagnostics give us a head start. Not a full diagnosis. We confirm a fault with tests at the unit, as part of our [fault finding](/heat-pump-troubleshooting/).

## How remote diagnostics help us fix a heat pump fault

When something goes wrong, the app often holds clues. By the time anyone looks at the unit, those clues are gone.

- **When it stopped.** Did it stop at 3am on the coldest night of the week? Or the moment it was switched on? Those are different stories.
- **What the room temperature did.** Did the room slowly stop warming over a fortnight? That points to something gradual, like a leak or a blocked coil. Not a part that suddenly died.
- **Whether a setting changed.** Someone else at home may have changed a schedule, a temperature limit or the mode in the app. That can look just like a fault.
- **Any fault notice.** If the app shows an error or alert, take a screenshot before you reset anything.

Send us those screenshots when you book. Add the brand and model number. Often we turn up with a clearer idea of where to start, and the right part in the van.

## Getting monitoring working on your heat pump

Mitsubishi Electric, Daikin and Fujitsu all offer Wi-Fi control on selected models. Some older units can have an adaptor added later. Here's what keeps it reliable.

- **Signal at the indoor unit.** The adaptor sits inside the heat pump. That's often high on an outside wall, a long way from the router. A weak signal there means dropouts.
- **The right band.** Lots of heat pump adaptors only use 2.4 GHz. Mesh systems and new routers sometimes need a setting changed so the adaptor can join.
- **Pair it again after changes.** New router, new Wi-Fi name or new password? Connect the adaptor again through the app.
- **One account for the house.** Set the app up under a shared household email. Then whoever looks after the heating can get in.

If your heat pump suits it, we can check it and set up the adaptor while we're there for a service or repair.

## Monitoring energy use

If your app shows energy figures, check them month to month. A big jump, with no change in how you use it, can be the first sign of trouble. A dirty coil, a part on its way out, or a leak. For a closer look at running costs, [energy consumption monitoring](/energy-consumption-monitoring/) tracks use over time.
