## What decides heat pump running costs

A heat pump's running cost is the power it draws multiplied by what you pay for that power. The power it draws depends on how hard it has to work to hold your rooms at the temperature you've set. Several things decide that.

- **Size match to the room.** A unit that's too small runs flat out and still struggles on a cold morning. One that's too big cycles on and off and doesn't run efficiently either.
- **Insulation and draughts.** A well insulated, draught-free room holds its heat, so the unit ticks over instead of working hard.
- **Set temperature.** The higher you set it, the more heat the unit has to deliver and the more power it uses.
- **How you run it.** Fan setting, whether you leave it on low or switch it off and on, and how clean it is all matter.
- **The unit's efficiency rating.** A more efficient model delivers the same heat for less power.
- **The weather.** Frosty Christchurch mornings make the unit work harder than mild ones.

We'll go through each of these, then show you how to work out your own cost.

## Heat pump running costs and sizing

The single biggest factor is whether the heat pump suits the room. The rated heating output on the label is measured at 7°C outside. Christchurch's cold mornings are well below that, so a unit chosen on its 7°C figure can come up short when you need it most.

When we [size a heat pump](/heat-pump-sizing/), we work from the room's floor area, ceiling height, glazing, insulation and how cold it gets outside. A unit matched to the room runs steadily at part speed and holds the temperature without straining. That's cheaper to run than one that's always catching up.

If you're not sure what size you need, the [single room heat pump calculator](/calculator-single-room-heat-pump/) gives you a starting figure.

## Heat pump running costs and insulation

A heat pump replaces the heat a room loses. The faster a room loses heat, the harder the unit works. Two things drive that loss: how well the house is insulated, and how much cold air gets in.

- **Ceiling and underfloor insulation** slow heat escaping through the roof and floor.
- **Draughts** around doors, windows and downlights let cold air in and warm air out.
- **Single glazing** loses heat fast, especially after dark.

Deal with the draughts and insulation first. Then the heat pump has less work to do. A [home heating assessment](https://tools.tenancy.govt.nz/heating-tool) can help you see where a room stands. For bigger improvements, look at [heat pump efficiency upgrades](/heat-pump-efficiency-upgrades/).

## Heat pump running costs and the temperature you set

The set temperature is the easiest thing to change. Around **18 to 21°C** is comfortable for most people in winter, and every degree above that adds to the running cost. Pick a setting you're happy with and leave it there. Nudging it up and down through the day doesn't help.

A heat pump heats to the temperature you set. If you set it higher hoping it warms up faster, it doesn't. It just keeps going past the point you actually wanted. So set the temperature you want, not a higher one.

## Best heat pump settings winter

These settings get the most heat for the least power.

- **Set temperature around 18 to 21°C.** Comfortable and not wasteful.
- **Use auto fan.** It adjusts the fan speed to the room, so the unit isn't blasting when it doesn't need to.
- **Point the louvres down in heating.** Warm air rises, so aim it at the floor to spread heat through the room.
- **Leave it on low rather than switching it off and on.** A cold house takes a lot of power to bring back up to temperature. Running steadily at a lower setting is usually cheaper.
- **Close doors to rooms you're not heating.** Heat the rooms you use.
- **Don't block the indoor or outdoor unit.** Air has to move freely over both coils.

## Heat pump running costs when you leave it on

This is the question we get most. The answer depends on how long you're away.

For a few hours, turn the heat pump down a degree or two rather than off. The house keeps some of its heat, and the unit doesn't have to work hard to bring it back. For a whole day away, switching off is fine.

What you want to avoid is the on and off cycle. Each time you switch on a cold house, the unit runs flat out to recover. That burst uses more power than holding a steady temperature would have. A unit that's the right size and left to run steadily is the cheapest way to heat.

## Heat pump running costs and clean filters

A heat pump moves heat by moving air over its coils. If the filters are clogged or the coil is dusty, air can't get through. The unit has to work harder to move the same heat, and its power use goes up.

- **Clean the filters** as often as once a month when you're using the unit a lot.
- **Have the coils cleaned** at a yearly [service](/maintenance-servicing/). The outdoor coil collects leaves, dust and, on the coast, salt.
- **Keep the outdoor unit clear** of plants and stored items.

A clean unit runs closer to its rated efficiency. It's the cheapest maintenance you can do.

## Heat pump running costs on frosty mornings

On a cold Christchurch morning, the outdoor coil has less heat to collect, so the unit works harder for the same output. Below about 7°C, moisture in the air can freeze on the coil, and the unit defrosts to melt it. No heat reaches the room while it does.

A unit sized for Christchurch's cold mornings copes better than an undersized one, which has to defrost more often and still struggles. If your heat pump seems to spend a lot of time defrosting, it may be undersized or low on refrigerant. See [winterisation of heat pumps](/winterisation-of-heat-pumps/) for what to check.

## Heat pump running costs and the efficiency rating

Every heat pump sold in New Zealand carries an energy rating label. It shows the unit's efficiency, so you can compare models. A more efficient unit delivers the same heat for less power, which lowers running costs over the unit's life.

The rating is measured at standard conditions, so real-world figures vary. But it's a fair guide when you're choosing between models. Look at [energy star heat pumps](/energy-star-heat-pumps/) to see how the rating works and what to compare.

## Working out heat pump power usage

We don't quote dollar figures, because your cost depends on your power price and how you run the unit. Here's how to work it out yourself.

1. **Find the rated input power.** It's on the unit's label or in the manual. It's the power the unit draws, in kW. Note the heating figure.
2. **Estimate the hours you run it.** Say how many hours a day and how many days a month.
3. **Multiply.** Rated input power (kW) times hours run gives you the energy used, in kWh.
4. **Multiply by your power price.** Your electricity bill shows what you pay per kWh. Energy used times price gives you the cost.

That's an estimate. It assumes the unit runs at its rated input, which it won't always. On mild days it draws less; on frosty mornings more. To get a truer picture, [monitor your energy consumption](/energy-consumption-monitoring/) and compare it with your bill.

## How much does a heat pump cost to run?

Running costs come down to a unit that suits the room, a house that holds its heat, sensible settings and a clean unit. If your heat pump seems to cost more than it should, the place to start is a service and a check that it's the right size for the room.
