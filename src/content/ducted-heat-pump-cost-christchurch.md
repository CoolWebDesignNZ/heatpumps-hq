## Why ducted heat pump prices vary so much

Ducted heat pump prices vary so much because every part of the system is sized to the house: a central indoor unit, a duct to each room, a return air path, controls, an outdoor unit and the power to run it. A wall heat pump is one unit for one room. A ducted heat pump is a small building services project.

That's why we don't publish ducted prices or ranges. The same system can be a very different job in a single-level new build in Rolleston and a two-storey 1970s house in Cashmere. A free site visit gives you a fixed price for yours.

## How system capacity affects ducted heat pump cost

The more capacity a ducted system needs, the more it costs, because larger units cost more. The indoor and outdoor units are sized to heat every room on the system at Christchurch's winter design temperature, and a bigger house, more glass, a high stud or poor insulation all push up the capacity needed.

Manufacturers split their ducted ranges by house size and power supply. GENERAL New Zealand, which sells the Fujitsu range, groups its ducted units on its [New Zealand site](https://www.generalairstage.co.nz/) into apartments and smaller homes up to four bedrooms, and medium to large homes up to six bedrooms, with single phase and three phase options.

## Single phase or three phase power

Your power supply matters because some of the larger ducted units come in three phase versions, as GENERAL's list shows, and a house is supplied with either single phase or three phase power. If the right unit for the house needs a supply the house doesn't have, that changes the electrical work considerably. We check the supply and switchboard as part of the quote, and the connection is always done by a licensed electrical worker.

## Number of rooms and outlets

Every room you add to a ducted system adds to the cost, because each needs a supply outlet in the ceiling (or floor) and a duct from the unit. Large rooms and open plan areas may need two outlets. More outlets means more duct, more fittings and more labour. The return air grille, where air is drawn back to the unit, has to go somewhere central with a clear path back from each room.

## Roof space, ceiling depth and duct runs

Roof space and ceiling depth decide how easily the indoor unit and ducts fit. Low-pitched roofs, tight trusses, a skillion roof or a ceiling that's mostly occupied by downlights and services all make the install harder.

Manufacturers make units for tighter spaces. Daikin's [New Zealand ducted range](https://www.daikin.co.nz/pages/ducted-heat-pumps), for example, includes a slim-line series for installations where ceiling space is limited, and a model with a depth of 700mm designed for New Zealand homes. Slimmer units can be the difference between a straightforward install and one that needs more work, but they come in specific capacities.

Long duct runs lose some air and heat, so the layout aims to keep them short and well insulated. A central unit position helps.

## Underfloor ducted installs

Where the roof space is unusable, some houses with a good crawl space can take the unit and ducts under the floor, with outlets in the floor. Access, clearance and moisture under the house decide whether that's practical.

## Zoning and the cost of a ducted heat pump

Zoning adds a controller, dampers and wiring to the cost of a ducted heat pump, and it can lower running costs afterwards. A zone controller with motorised dampers lets you heat the living areas in the day and the bedrooms at night, rather than the whole house at once. Daikin and Mitsubishi Electric both list zone controllers on their NZ sites. See [heat pump zoning](/heat-pump-zoning/).

## Ducted heat pump cost in a new build or retrofit

A new build keeps a ducted heat pump job simpler than a retrofit. Ducts can go in before the ceilings are lined and the insulation is laid, and the outdoor unit, power supply and return air position can be planned with the builder.

In an existing home the installer works around what's there: lifting and replacing ceiling insulation, cutting outlets into finished ceilings, finding a route through framing and making good afterwards. Two-storey homes add the problem of reaching upstairs and downstairs from one system, which can mean bulkheads or a second unit.

## The cost of replacing an old ducted heat pump

Swapping an old ducted system for a new one depends on what can be reused. Things we check:

- the condition and insulation of the existing ducts
- whether the ducts are sized for the new unit's airflow
- the position of the old unit and outdoor unit
- the existing refrigerant pipework and what refrigerant the old system used
- the power supply and isolating switch.

Old refrigerant has to be recovered, not released. Some manufacturers make units designed to replace old R22 ducted systems. Daikin's NZ site lists retrofit capability for one of its inverter ducted series, for example.

## Ducted outdoor unit position

Ducted outdoor units are often larger than single-room units. They need clear airflow, a solid base or brackets, service access and a spot that keeps noise at the boundary within limits. Those limits are in the [heat pump boundary rules](/heat-pump-boundary-rules-consent-nz/).

## Adding fresh air and ventilation

Some ducted systems can be paired with a heat recovery ventilation unit. It's an extra system with its own cost, but it can share the planning if you're doing both.

## What a ducted heat pump price should include

A ducted heat pump price should spell out:

- brand, model and heating capacity of the indoor and outdoor units
- the number and position of outlets and the return air grille
- the indoor unit position and how it's supported
- duct type and insulation
- zone controller and dampers, if included
- electrical work, including the supply and isolating switch
- removal of any old system and refrigerant recovery
- how the system was sized.
