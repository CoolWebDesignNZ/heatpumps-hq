## ENERGY STAR heat pumps and the New Zealand label

In New Zealand the label to compare is the Zoned Energy Rating Label, not the blue ENERGY STAR mark. Someone looking for an energy star heat pump may mean either one: the ENERGY STAR mark, or the star rating printed on the energy label. They're different.

In New Zealand, heat pumps are covered by Minimum Energy Performance Standards and mandatory energy labelling, regulated by EECA under the Energy Efficiency (Energy Using Products) Regulations 2002. Every heat pump model new to the market since 1 July 2021 has had to carry the **Zoned Energy Rating Label**.

Source for this page: [EECA, The new Zoned Energy Rating Label for heat pumps and air conditioners](https://www.eeca.govt.nz/assets/EECA-Resources/Product-regulations/The-new-Zoned-Energy-Rating-Label-for-heat-pumps-air-conditioners.pdf).

## How to read a heat pump's Zoned Energy Rating Label

The Zoned Energy Rating Label has five parts to read: the zone map, the heating and cooling stars, the kWh per year figures, the capacity figures and the noise figures.

### The map and the zones

The label shows a map of Australia and New Zealand split into three climate zones, each with its own row of stars. New Zealand is entirely in the **Cold** zone. The other two zones are for parts of Australia, so you can ignore them.

### Heating and cooling stars

Each zone has separate heating stars and cooling stars. In Christchurch you'll use a heat pump far more for heating, so the **Cold zone heating stars** are the figure to compare.

The stars compare a heat pump with others of similar capacity. More stars means better efficiency. EECA describes a one star unit as one of the poorest performers for its size, with a seasonal performance factor of 2.5 to 3, while a three star unit sits between 4.5 and 5. When the label was developed, the best unit reached eight stars for cooling in one zone, which leaves room for future models to improve.

### The kWh per year figures

At the right hand edge of each star row is a kWh per year figure for heating and one for cooling. They indicate how much electricity the heat pump may use in that zone over a year. EECA is clear that they are only an indication, because every household uses a heat pump differently. They're most useful for comparing two models side by side. To estimate a running cost, EECA suggests adding the heating and cooling kWh for your zone and multiplying by your own electricity tariff. Our [heat pump power usage page](/energy-consumption-monitoring/) explains why your real figure will differ.

### The capacity figures

Near the top of the label are three capacity numbers: cooling at 35°C outside, and heating at 7°C and at 2°C outside. Size the unit on the 7°C heating figure if heating is its main job. If your area gets overnight winter temperatures of 2°C or less, EECA advises checking that the 2°C heating figure is the same as or greater than the 7°C one. Our [cold climate guide](/cold-climate-heat-pumps/) covers why that number matters.

### The noise figures

A house icon at the bottom can show indoor and outdoor sound levels, though showing noise is optional in New Zealand. Our [heat pump noise guide](/heat-pump-noise-problems-solutions/) explains how those figures are measured and how to use them.

## What the energy star rating doesn't tell you

The energy star rating doesn't tell you how a heat pump will perform in your house. It's a fair comparison between similar sized units under test conditions, but it doesn't account for:

- **Sizing.** A five star unit that's too small for the room will run flat out and still leave it cold.
- **Installation.** A boxed-in outdoor unit or a poorly placed indoor unit loses efficiency.
- **Your house.** Insulation, glazing and draughts set how much heat you need.
- **Servicing.** Dirty coils and filters cut real-world performance over time.

That's why we use the label as one input, alongside a proper sizing of your rooms. See [what makes an energy efficient heat pump](/energy-efficient/).

## Old labels and older units

You may see heat pumps with the older Energy Rating Label, especially on existing installs or second-hand units. EECA says the stars on that label measured something different and can't be converted to the new ones. If you're comparing an old heat pump with a new one, compare their actual capacity and age, not their stars.
