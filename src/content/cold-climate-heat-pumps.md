## Why cold weather changes the choice

A heat pump heats your house by pulling heat out of the outdoor air. The colder that air gets, the harder the job. Two things happen on a frosty Canterbury night:

- **The house needs more heat just as the air has less to give.** Some models keep up their output as it gets colder. Others fall away.
- **Frost forms on the outdoor coil.** EECA explains that once the outside temperature is below 5.5°C, the unit has to deal with frosting conditions and divert some of its heating capacity to defrost the coil.

A cold climate heat pump is one that copes with both. It keeps enough output to hold the room at temperature on the coldest mornings, and it defrosts without leaving the room cold for long.

## What to look for

### The 2°C heating figure on the label

New heat pump models sold in New Zealand carry the Zoned Energy Rating Label. It shows the heating capacity at two outdoor temperatures, 7°C and 2°C. EECA's guidance is that if you live where overnight winter temperatures get down to 2°C or less, you should check that the 2°C heating capacity is the same as or greater than the 7°C figure, and use it to compare models. Some units are not well designed to cope with frost and give less heat at 2°C than at 7°C.

Source: [EECA, The new Zoned Energy Rating Label for heat pumps and air conditioners](https://www.eeca.govt.nz/assets/EECA-Resources/Product-regulations/The-new-Zoned-Energy-Rating-Label-for-heat-pumps-air-conditioners.pdf).

### Heating stars for the Cold zone

On the label, all of New Zealand sits in the Cold zone. The heating stars in that zone show how efficient the unit is compared with others of similar capacity. More stars means less electricity for the same heat. Our [energy rating label guide](/energy-star-heat-pumps/) walks through every part of the label.

### The heating operating range

Manufacturers state the lowest outdoor temperature a model will keep heating at. Ranges differ a lot between models, so check the exact model rather than the brand. As one example, Mitsubishi Electric says its HyperCore heat pumps have an extended heating range that allows heating to minus 25°C, and that it guarantees full rated heating capacity down to minus 15°C on certain models ([Mitsubishi Electric NZ, HyperCore technology](https://www.mitsubishi-electric.co.nz/publications/heat-pump/Heat-Pump-HyperCore-Technology-South-Island)). Note the difference between the two figures: operating at a temperature is not the same as giving full output at it.

### A base heater or de-icer

Some outdoor units have a heater in the drain tray that stops ice building up in the base. Mitsubishi Electric says the de-icer on its HyperCore units doesn't add heat to the room and won't run in most conditions above 0°C. It's useful where frosts are heavy and regular.

## Sizing for the coldest mornings

When a heat pump struggles in winter, the brand is rarely the problem. More often the unit was chosen for an average day rather than a cold one. When we size an install we work from the rooms, the insulation, the windows and how the house is used, then check the chosen unit's output at low outdoor temperatures, not just its headline kW.

That matters most in rooms with high ceilings, big glass, open stairwells or poor insulation, where the heat loss on a frosty morning is well above what the room needs on a mild day. The [heat pump sizing guide](/heat-pump-sizing/) covers how that calculation works.

## Placing the outdoor unit for frost

Where the outdoor unit sits affects how well it handles cold weather:

- **Raised off the ground.** Feet or a wall bracket keep the coil clear of ground frost and let ice fall away from the base.
- **A clear drain path.** Defrost produces meltwater. It needs to drain to garden or gravel, not onto a path, deck or driveway where it freezes.
- **Out of drips and drifts.** A unit under a roof edge with no spouting gets dripped on and iced up. Keep it clear of where snow slides off the roof.
- **Open air around it.** A unit boxed in by fences or a tight cover pulls back its own cold discharge air, which means more frost and more defrost cycles. See our [heat pump covers guide](/best-heat-pump-covers-nz/) for screens that leave enough space.

## Inland frosts, hills and the coast

Frosts tend to be harder out on the plains than near the coast. If you're in Rolleston, Lincoln, Prebbleton or on a rural block, plan for the colder end. On the hills and the coast, wind can matter as much as frost, so keep the outdoor unit out of the prevailing wind where you can.
