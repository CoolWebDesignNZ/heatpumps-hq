## How low can a heat pump go?

There is no single answer, because every model has its own heating operating range. The manufacturer sets a lowest outdoor temperature at which the unit will keep heating, and it's in the specification sheet for that model. Some Mitsubishi Electric models are rated to heat down to minus 25°C ([Mitsubishi Electric NZ](https://www.mitsubishi-electric.co.nz/publications/heat-pump/Heat-Pump-HyperCore-Technology-South-Island)).

That figure is a limit, not a promise of full output. Two things happen as the temperature falls well before the limit is reached.

## What happens below zero

### Output drops

A heat pump moves heat from the outdoor air into the house. The colder the outdoor air, the more work it takes to move each unit of heat. Some models hold their output well as the temperature falls; others give less heat on a frosty morning than on a mild afternoon, just when the house needs more. That's why sizing for cold mornings matters.

### Frost forms and the unit defrosts

EECA notes that below 5.5°C outside, a heat pump has to deal with frosting conditions, and some of its heating capacity goes into defrosting the coil ([EECA, Zoned Energy Rating Label guide](https://www.eeca.govt.nz/assets/EECA-Resources/Product-regulations/The-new-Zoned-Energy-Rating-Label-for-heat-pumps-air-conditioners.pdf)). During a defrost the unit is not delivering heat to the room. You'll notice:

- the indoor fan slowing or stopping, and an indicator light on some models
- steam or vapour rising off the outdoor unit
- a hiss or whoosh as the refrigerant flow reverses
- water draining out of the bottom of the outdoor unit

After a few minutes it switches back and the warm air returns. On a cold, damp morning it can happen again and again. That's normal.

## When it's not working in cold weather

If the house stays cold and the heat pump seems to be giving up, work through these before you call:

1. **Check the mode and set temperature.** Make sure it's on heat, not auto or dry, and the set temperature is above the room temperature.
2. **Clean the filters.** Clogged filters cut airflow, and less airflow means less heat out of the indoor unit. It takes a few minutes.
3. **Look at the outdoor unit.** Is it buried in leaves, snow or ice? Is anything blocking the back or sides, such as a wheelie bin, firewood stack or overgrown hedge? Clear it.
4. **Watch for ice in the base.** If meltwater can't drain away, it refreezes in the base tray and around the fan. Clear the drain path below the unit. Don't chip ice off the coil, because the thin metal fins bend and puncture easily.
5. **Give it time after a defrost.** If it's been off for a few minutes and the outdoor unit is steaming, wait for the cycle to finish.
6. **Reset it once.** Turn it off at the isolating switch beside the outdoor unit for five minutes, then back on.

If it still isn't heating, or it's showing a flashing light or error code, note the code and call. A unit that blows cool air for long stretches with no sign of a defrost, a coil that stays solid with ice, or an outdoor fan that won't turn are all faults rather than cold weather behaviour. ## Why some heat pumps struggle more than others

When a heat pump works fine in autumn and falls short every July, the cause is usually one of these:

- **It was sized for an average day.** The unit's output at low outdoor temperatures was never enough for the room on a frosty morning.
- **It's an older or basic model.** Its heating range and low temperature capacity are narrower than a current cold climate model.
- **The outdoor unit is in a poor spot.** Boxed in, in a frost hollow, or where it pulls back its own cold air.
- **It needs a service.** A dirty outdoor coil or a slightly low refrigerant charge tends to show up in the coldest weeks.

A service before winter rules out the last one. If the unit is undersized or old, we'll tell you whether a second unit, a larger unit or a replacement makes more sense. Our [cold climate heat pump guide](/cold-climate-heat-pumps/) covers what to look for in a new one.

## Settings for a cold snap

- **Keep it running steadily.** Starting from a cold house on the coldest part of the morning asks the most of the unit when it has the least to give.
- **Use the timer.** Have it start before you get up rather than switching it on high when you're already cold.
- **Leave the fan on auto.** The unit can then raise the airflow when it needs to put out more heat.
- **Close doors to rooms you aren't heating.** The heat goes where you need it.
