## The official calculator

The free tool to use is the **[Tenancy Services heating assessment tool](https://tools.tenancy.govt.nz/heating-tool)**, run by the Ministry of Business, Innovation and Employment. It calculates the minimum heating capacity, in kW, that a main living room needs under the Healthy Homes Standards. Tenancy Services describes it on its [heating capacity page](https://www.tenancy.govt.nz/healthy-homes/heating-standard/calculating-heating-capacity/).

It was built for landlords, but it isn't only for rentals. EECA's [good practice guide for heat pump installation](https://www.eeca.govt.nz/assets/EECA-Resources/Good-Practice-Guide-Heat-Pump-Installation-March-2022.pdf) tells installers to use this tool, or the formula behind it, to size heat pumps for the living room in any home and for other rooms such as bedrooms.

## What the calculator asks

According to the [Tenancy Services tool guide](https://www.tenancy.govt.nz/heating-tool/guide/), it asks about:

- **the region** the home is in, to set the local climate
- **the type of property and when it received building consent**, which decides which of two formulas applies
- **how much space you need to heat**
- **the surfaces heat can escape through**: walls, windows, doors, floor and ceiling
- **how well those surfaces are insulated.**

The result comes out in kilowatts, rounded up to one decimal place. You can save your progress with an email address and come back to it.

## What to measure before you start

Have these ready and the tool takes far less time:

1. **Floor area of the room.** Length times width in metres. For an L-shaped room, split it into rectangles and add them.
2. **Ceiling height.** Measure at the highest point if the ceiling slopes.
3. **External walls.** The length and height of each wall that faces outside. Internal walls to heated rooms lose much less heat.
4. **Windows and glass doors.** The size of each one, and whether it's single or double glazed.
5. **Doors.** External doors and any opening into an unheated space, such as a garage or a cold hallway.
6. **The floor.** Timber over a crawl space or a concrete slab, and whether there's underfloor insulation.
7. **The ceiling.** Whether there's a roof space above, and how much insulation is up there. Lifting the hatch and looking is better than guessing.
8. **Wall insulation.** If you don't know, the build date and any renovation records help.

The tool guide's advice is that the more accurate the information, the more accurate the report. Where it gives a default estimate, replace it with a real figure if you can.

## Where the calculator gets it wrong

The tool guide is open about its limits:

- **Sloped or irregular ceilings.** The tool assumes a 45 degree slope and works out the room volume using the highest wall height, as if the room were a box. That usually overestimates what the room needs.
- **Sloped external walls** may also be overestimated.
- **Complex layouts.** For rooms that are hard to measure, Tenancy Services recommends a professional, such as a home performance assessor, who can apply the formula in Schedule 2 of the Healthy Homes regulations directly.

The calculator also doesn't know about things it doesn't ask: a room that gets afternoon sun, a draughty old fireplace, or an open stairwell that pulls warm air upstairs.

## What to do with the result

The kW number is the heat the room needs at the cold design temperature for your area. It is **not** the rated capacity to look for on the box.

The heating figure shown on a heat pump's label is its output at 7°C outside. EECA's installation guide lists Christchurch's design temperature as minus 4°C, and at that temperature many units deliver well under their rating. To match a unit to your result:

1. Take the calculator's kW figure.
2. Look at the manufacturer's performance chart for the model, which shows output at low outdoor temperatures.
3. Pick a model whose output at the nearest temperature at or below minus 4°C meets or exceeds your figure.

If a model's chart isn't available, EECA's [energy label guidance](https://www.eeca.govt.nz/assets/EECA-Resources/Product-regulations/The-new-Zoned-Energy-Rating-Label-for-heat-pumps-air-conditioners.pdf) suggests at least checking that the 2°C heating figure on the label holds up against the 7°C figure. ## Brand sizing tools

Heat pump brands run their own calculators. Mitsubishi Electric's [sizing tool](https://www.mitsubishi-electric.co.nz/heatpump/how-to-choose-the-right-size-heat-pump.aspx), for example, asks for the room's width, length and height, how well insulated it is, and the nearest region, then suggests models from its range. Tools like this are quick for a first look, but they answer a different question: which of their models might suit, rather than how much heat the room loses.

## When a calculator isn't enough

A calculation is only as good as the inputs, and some rooms don't fit a form. Get the room assessed on site when:

- the living area is open plan, or runs into a kitchen, dining room or stairwell
- the ceiling is raked, or there's a mezzanine
- you're choosing between one larger unit and two smaller ones
- you're replacing a wood burner that heated more than one room
- the outdoor unit position is awkward, which can affect which models are practical.
