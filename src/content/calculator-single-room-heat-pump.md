## The official heat pump size calculator

The free tool to use is the **[Tenancy Services heating assessment tool](https://tools.tenancy.govt.nz/heating-tool)**. The Ministry of Business, Innovation and Employment runs it. It works out the minimum heating capacity, in kW, that a main living room needs under the Healthy Homes Standards. Tenancy Services explains [how heating capacity is calculated](https://www.tenancy.govt.nz/healthy-homes/heating-standard/calculating-heating-capacity/).

It was built for landlords. But it isn't only for rentals. EECA's [good practice guide for heat pump installation](https://www.eeca.govt.nz/assets/EECA-Resources/Good-Practice-Guide-Heat-Pump-Installation-March-2022.pdf) tells installers to use it, or the formula behind it. That goes for the living room in any home, and for other rooms like bedrooms.

## What the heat pump calculator asks

The [Tenancy Services tool guide](https://www.tenancy.govt.nz/heating-tool/guide/) says it asks about these things:

- **The region** your home is in. That sets the local climate.
- **The type of property and when it got building consent.** That decides which of two formulas applies.
- **How much space you need to heat.** More space takes more heat.
- **The surfaces heat can escape through.** Walls, windows, doors, floor and ceiling.
- **How well those surfaces are insulated.** Better insulation means less heat lost through them.

You get the result in kilowatts, rounded up to one decimal place. You can save your progress with an email address and come back to it later.

## What to measure for a heat pump room size calculator

You'll need the room's measurements and how it's built. Have these ready and it's much quicker:

1. **Floor area.** Length times width in metres. For an L-shaped room, split it into rectangles and add them up.
2. **Ceiling height.** If it slopes, measure at the highest point.
3. **External walls.** The length and height of each wall facing outside. Walls to other heated rooms lose much less heat.
4. **Windows and glass doors.** The size of each one. Note whether it's single or double glazed.
5. **Doors.** Outside doors, and any opening into a cold space like a garage or hallway.
6. **The floor.** Timber over a crawl space, or a concrete slab? Is there underfloor insulation?
7. **The ceiling.** Is there a roof space above? How much insulation is up there? Lift the hatch and look. It beats guessing.
8. **Wall insulation.** If you don't know, the build date and any renovation records help.

The tool guide puts it simply. The better your information, the better the report. Where it gives a default estimate, swap in a real figure if you can.

## Where the calculator gets it wrong

The calculator struggles with sloped ceilings, sloped walls and tricky layouts. The Tenancy Services tool guide says so itself:

- **Sloped or odd-shaped ceilings.** The tool assumes a 45 degree slope. It works out the room volume from the highest wall, as if the room were a box. That usually overstates what the room needs.
- **Sloped external walls.** These may be overstated too.
- **Tricky layouts.** For rooms that are hard to measure, Tenancy Services recommends a professional. A home performance assessor, say, can apply the formula in Schedule 2 of the Healthy Homes regulations directly.

The calculator also misses things it doesn't ask about, like a room that gets afternoon sun, a draughty old fireplace, or an open stairwell pulling warm air upstairs.

## What to do with the heat pump kW calculator result

The kW number is the heat your room needs at the cold design temperature for your area. It's **not** the rated capacity to look for on the box.

The heating figure on a heat pump's label is its output at 7°C outside. EECA's installation guide puts Christchurch's design temperature at minus 4°C. At that temperature, lots of units put out well under their rating. To match a unit to your result:

1. Take the calculator's kW figure.
2. Look at the maker's performance chart for the model. It shows output at low outdoor temperatures.
3. Pick a model whose output meets or beats your figure at the nearest temperature at or below minus 4°C.

Can't find the chart? EECA's [energy label guidance](https://www.eeca.govt.nz/assets/EECA-Resources/Product-regulations/The-new-Zoned-Energy-Rating-Label-for-heat-pumps-air-conditioners.pdf) suggests at least checking the 2°C heating figure on the label holds up against the 7°C one. We walk through EECA's worked example in [heat pump sizing](/heat-pump-sizing/).

## Brand sizing tools

Heat pump brands run their own calculators. Mitsubishi Electric's [sizing tool](https://www.mitsubishi-electric.co.nz/heatpump/how-to-choose-the-right-size-heat-pump.aspx) is one. It asks for the room's width, length and height, how well it's insulated and the nearest region. Then it suggests models from its range. Tools like this are quick for a first look. But they answer a different question. They tell you which of their models might suit. They don't tell you how much heat your room loses.

## When a calculator isn't enough

A calculation is only as good as what goes in. And some rooms don't fit a form. Get the room looked at on site when:

- **Open plan:** the living area runs into a kitchen, dining room or stairwell.
- **Odd ceilings:** it's raked, or there's a mezzanine.
- **One or two units:** you're torn between one bigger unit and two smaller ones.
- **Wood burner swap:** the old burner heated more than one room.
- **Tricky outdoor spot:** an awkward spot for the outdoor unit can limit which models are practical.
