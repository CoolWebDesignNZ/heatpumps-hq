## The short answer on heat pump boundary rules

The Christchurch District Plan controls heat pumps through **noise**, not distance. Any activity that makes noise, including a heat pump outdoor unit, has to meet the zone noise limits at the site receiving the noise. There is no rule that a heat pump must be a set number of metres from the fence.

That means where the outdoor unit goes is a judgement about sound: how loud the unit is, how close it is to the neighbour's bedroom windows, and what's around it.

## Heat pump noise rules in residential zones

In residential zones outside the Central City, heat pump noise at the receiving site must stay under 50 dB LAeq by day and 40 dB LAeq and 65 dB LAmax at night. The District Plan sets the limits by time of day (Rule 6.1.5.2.1, Table 1):

| Time | Limit |
| --- | --- |
| 7am to 10pm | 50 dB LAeq |
| 10pm to 7am | 40 dB LAeq and 65 dB LAmax |

The night-time limit is the one to plan for. Heat pumps run hardest on cold nights and early mornings, which is exactly when the 40 dB limit applies. The Central City and commercial zones have different limits.

Source: [Christchurch District Plan, Chapter 6.1 Noise](https://www.proposeddistrictplan1.ccc.govt.nz/assets/Documents/proposed-Christchurch-Replacement-District-Plan/Decision-63-Schedule-1-Chapter-6.1-6.11-Optimized.pdf) (decision version). Check the current text at [districtplan.ccc.govt.nz](https://districtplan.ccc.govt.nz/) before relying on a figure.

## Selwyn and Waimakariri are different

If you live in Rolleston, Lincoln, Prebbleton or anywhere else in Selwyn, the Selwyn District Plan applies, not Christchurch's. Kaiapoi and Rangiora are under Waimakariri District Council, which publishes its own [fact sheet on noisy heat pumps](https://www.waimakariri.govt.nz/__data/assets/pdf_file/0024/181194/QD-ENV-Information-012-Noisy-Heat-Pumps.PDF). The principle is the same everywhere: noise is measured at the neighbour's property.

## Where to put the outdoor unit near a boundary

Near a boundary, put the outdoor unit where it stays quiet at the neighbour's property:

- **Away from the neighbour's bedrooms.** Put the unit beside your own garage, laundry or a blank wall rather than facing their bedroom windows.
- **Out of corners.** A unit tucked into a corner between two walls bounces sound back out. Open wall space is quieter.
- **On anti-vibration feet or brackets.** Rubber feet stop the hum travelling into the house and the ground.
- **Screened, not boxed in.** A slatted timber screen helps with looks and some sound, but the unit needs open space around it to breathe. A tight box makes it work harder and louder.
- **The right size unit.** An undersized unit runs flat out every night. A correctly sized one cruises. See our [heat pump sizing guide](/heat-pump-sizing/).

## When a heat pump needs consent

A heat pump that meets the District Plan noise limits is a permitted activity, so no resource consent is needed. In practice nearly every domestic install falls into this group.

Consent only becomes a question when a unit can't meet the limits, for example a large unit squeezed hard against a neighbour's bedroom wall. The usual answer is to move or change the unit rather than apply for consent.

A standard residential split system is normally installed without building consent. The electrical connection still has to be done by a licensed electrical worker.

## When a neighbour complains

When a neighbour complains, Christchurch City Council investigates. If measurements show a heat pump is over the limit, the council can issue an abatement notice requiring the noise to be reduced. A Christchurch family's dispute over this went as far as the Environment Court, [as RNZ reported](https://www.rnz.co.nz/news/regions/573730/family-s-battle-with-council-over-noisy-heat-pump-reaches-environment-court), before the council cancelled the notice after further monitoring.
