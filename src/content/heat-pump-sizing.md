## What heat pump size means in kW

Every heat pump has a **heating capacity** and a **cooling capacity**, given in kilowatts (kW). That's the amount of heat it can deliver, not the electricity it uses. A unit rated at 6 kW heating can put about 6 kW of heat into a room under test conditions while drawing much less from the wall.

The catch is the test condition. On the Zoned Energy Rating Label the main heating figure is measured at **7°C outside**. The label also shows output at **2°C**, because many units give out less heat as the air gets colder. EECA's [guide to the label](https://www.eeca.govt.nz/assets/EECA-Resources/Product-regulations/The-new-Zoned-Energy-Rating-Label-for-heat-pumps-air-conditioners.pdf) says that if you live where overnight temperatures get down to 2°C or less, check that the 2°C figure is the same as or greater than the 7°C figure, and use it to compare models.

## Size for the cold, not the brochure

EECA's [good practice guide for heat pump installation](https://www.eeca.govt.nz/assets/EECA-Resources/Good-Practice-Guide-Heat-Pump-Installation-March-2022.pdf) sets out the method installers should follow:

1. **Work out the heating the room needs** (its heat load) in kW.
2. **Find the external design temperature** for the area. The guide lists it by council, taken from the Healthy Homes Standards. For **Christchurch City and Selwyn District it is minus 4°C**. Waimakariri is also minus 4°C.
3. **Choose a model that delivers the heat load at that temperature**, using the manufacturer's performance chart for output at low outdoor temperatures.

The guide adds that a site with a particularly severe microclimate, such as a sunless or damp valley, may need a design temperature a degree or two lower. In Christchurch that can apply to a shaded hillside section or a low-lying spot that holds frost.

### EECA's worked example

The guide gives an example for a site with a design temperature of +1°C, milder than Christchurch: a room needs **5.6 kW** of heat. At that temperature, a model rated at 6 kW only delivers 5.1 kW, which isn't enough. A model rated at 8 kW delivers 6.2 kW, so the 8 kW unit is the right choice. The rated number on the box was well above what the room needed, and the smaller unit still fell short.

A unit picked by its rated figure alone can look generous on paper and still fall behind on a July morning.

## What decides a room's heat load

The heat load is how fast the room loses heat when it's cold outside. EECA's checklist of building conditions includes:

- the orientation of the building and its windows, and how much sun they get
- the areas of the walls, ceiling, windows and floor
- air leaks and draughts around doors and windows
- insulation in the walls, ceiling and floor
- single or double glazing.

The checklist also asks what type of home it is (old, new, insulated), and whether it's a single room or a large open plan space or hallway where more than one unit may be needed.

There is no honest single figure for watts per square metre. Two rooms the same size can need very different units: a north-facing room in an insulated, double-glazed Rolleston house loses heat far more slowly than a high-stud villa room in Sydenham with single glazing and an open fireplace. Ceiling height counts too, because you're heating the volume of air, not the floor.

## What happens when heat pump sizing goes wrong

EECA's guide sets out what happens when the size is wrong:

| If the unit is | What happens | Effects |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Undersized | Runs at full output too often and defrosts too often | Room doesn't get warm, cold air during defrost, higher running costs, extra wear |
| Significantly oversized | Short cycles because output exceeds demand, higher start-up power | Higher running costs, draughts even on low fan, more noise, extra wear |

In Canterbury the bigger risk is undersizing, because output drops in the cold just when the room needs the most heat. A unit slightly above the calculated minimum gives a margin for the coldest mornings.

## What size heat pump do I need for open plan spaces?

For a big open plan area, one very large unit isn't always the answer. EECA's guide notes that two smaller units, or a multi split system, can spread heat better and give better control than a single high-capacity unit. A living area that runs into a kitchen and dining space, or a lounge with a long hallway off it, is a common case. See [multi-room installation](/multi-room-heat-pump-installation/).

## Rental homes

For the main living room of a rental, the Healthy Homes Standards set a minimum heating capacity. EECA recommends using the same calculation for living rooms in any home, so a heat pump would still comply if the house were rented later. ## Beyond an online heat pump size guide

Online estimates are a useful starting point, but they rely on your measurements and guesses about insulation.
