## What heat pump size means in kW

Every heat pump has a **heating capacity** and a **cooling capacity**, in kilowatts (kW). That's how much heat it can deliver. It's not the power it uses. A 6 kW heating unit can put about 6 kW of heat into a room under test conditions. And it draws much less than that from the wall.

The catch is the test conditions. On the Zoned Energy Rating Label, the main heating figure is measured at **7°C outside**. The label also shows output at **2°C**. That's because lots of units put out less heat as the air gets colder. EECA's [guide to the label](https://www.eeca.govt.nz/assets/EECA-Resources/Product-regulations/The-new-Zoned-Energy-Rating-Label-for-heat-pumps-air-conditioners.pdf) covers this. If your nights get down to 2°C or below, check the 2°C figure is the same as the 7°C figure, or higher. Then use it to compare models.

## Size for the cold, not the brochure

Pick a heat pump by the heat it gives on the coldest mornings where you live. Don't go by the rating on the box. EECA's [good practice guide for heat pump installation](https://www.eeca.govt.nz/assets/EECA-Resources/Good-Practice-Guide-Heat-Pump-Installation-March-2022.pdf) sets out the method installers should use:

1. **Work out the heat the room needs.** That's its heat load, in kW.
2. **Find the design temperature for your area.** The guide lists it by council, taken from the Healthy Homes Standards. For **Christchurch City and Selwyn District it is minus 4°C**. Waimakariri is minus 4°C as well.
3. **Pick a model that delivers the heat load at that temperature.** Use the maker's performance chart, which shows output at low outdoor temperatures.

The guide adds one more thing. A site with a harsh microclimate, like a sunless or damp valley, may need a design temperature a degree or two lower. In Christchurch, that can mean a shaded hillside section or a low spot that holds frost.

### EECA's worked example

The guide's example uses a site with a design temperature of +1°C. That's milder than Christchurch. The room needs **5.6 kW** of heat. At that temperature, a 6 kW model only gives 5.1 kW. That's not enough. An 8 kW model gives 6.2 kW, so the 8 kW unit is the right pick. The rating on the box was well above what the room needed. And the smaller unit still fell short.

Pick a unit on its rating alone and it can look generous on paper. Then it falls behind on a July morning.

## What decides a room's heat load

The heat load is how fast the room loses heat when it's cold outside. EECA's checklist covers these building details:

- **Sun:** which way the building and its windows face, and how much sun they get.
- **Areas:** the size of the walls, ceiling, windows and floor.
- **Draughts:** air leaks round doors and windows.
- **Insulation:** in the walls, ceiling and floor.
- **Glazing:** single or double.

The checklist also asks what kind of home it is: old, new or insulated. And it asks whether it's a single room or a big open plan space or hallway, where you might need more than one unit.

There's no honest single watts per square metre figure. Two rooms the same size can need very different units. Take a north-facing room in an insulated, double-glazed Rolleston house. It loses heat far more slowly than a high-stud villa room in Sydenham with single glazing and an open fireplace. Ceiling height counts too. You're heating the air in the room, not the floor.

## What happens when heat pump sizing goes wrong

Too small and the room stays cold and costs more to run. Much too big and it short cycles. That costs more too, and adds noise and wear. EECA's guide sets out both:

| If the unit is | What happens | Effects |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Undersized | Runs at full output too often and defrosts too often | Room doesn't get warm, cold air during defrost, higher running costs, extra wear |
| Significantly oversized | Short cycles because output exceeds demand, higher start-up power | Higher running costs, draughts even on low fan, more noise, extra wear |

In Canterbury, too small is the bigger risk. Output drops in the cold, right when the room needs the most heat. A unit a little above the calculated minimum gives you a margin for the coldest mornings.

## What size heat pump do I need for open plan spaces?

An open plan space often needs two smaller units or a multi split, not one very big unit. EECA's guide says two smaller units, or a multi split, can spread heat better. They give you better control too. A living area that runs into a kitchen and dining space is a common case. So is a lounge with a long hallway off it. ## Rental homes

For a rental's main living room, the Healthy Homes Standards set a minimum heating capacity. EECA recommends the same calculation for living rooms in any home. That way the heat pump still complies if you rent the house out later. The official Tenancy Services [heat pump size calculator](/calculator-single-room-heat-pump/) runs that calculation.
