## How to prepare your heat pump for winter

Do these in autumn, while the days are still mild:

1. **Wash the filters.** Summer dust and pollen sit in them all season.
2. **Run it in heat mode for half an hour.** You want to know it works before you need it.
3. **Check the outdoor unit's surroundings.** Clear leaves and weeds from under and around it. Trim back plants so air can move freely.
4. **Look at the feet or bracket.** The unit should sit off the ground, so defrost water can drain and the base doesn't freeze into a block of ice.
5. **Book a check if anything seems off.** An autumn [tune-up](/heat-pump-seasonal-tune-ups/) sorts small problems before the cold.

## Heat pump winter settings that work

The winter settings that work are heat mode, a steady temperature, the fan on auto, the louvres down, a timer and closed doors.

**Choose heat mode, not auto.** Auto can flick between heating and cooling on a mild winter afternoon. Heat mode keeps it doing one job.

**Pick a steady temperature.** Setting it very high doesn't warm the room much faster. The unit keeps working hard for longer, and the room overshoots. Choose a comfortable number and leave it.

**Fan on auto.** The unit works out the airflow it needs. Turn the fan up if you want the room warm quickly; it moves more warm air into the room.

**Louvres pointing down.** Warm air rises. Aim it at the floor and it will spread up through the room.

**Use the timer.** Set it to come on before you wake up or get home. A unit that starts from cold at full demand works harder than one that has been running gently.

**Close doors to unheated rooms.** A heat pump sized for a living area can't heat the whole house as well. Keep the warmth where it's needed. If you need more rooms heated, see [multi-room installation](/multi-room-heat-pump-installation/).

## The outdoor unit in a Canterbury winter

Through a Canterbury winter the outdoor unit deals with frost, meltwater, ice and the odd snowfall, and each needs handling differently.

**Frost and defrost.** On a cold, clear night, moisture in the air freezes on the outdoor coil. The unit reverses for a few minutes to melt it, and you'll see steam and hear a whoosh. The indoor fan pauses while this happens, so the room may feel cooler briefly. That's normal.

**Where the meltwater goes.** Defrost water drips from the base. On a concrete path it can freeze into a slippery patch overnight. If the unit sits above a path or a doorway, ask us about a drain tray or a gravel soak area.

**Ice that stays.** If ice builds up thicker each night, or the whole unit is encased, the defrost cycle isn't keeping up. That can be a sensor, low refrigerant or a unit sitting in a frozen puddle. Turn it off and call us. Don't chip the ice off; the fins bend easily.

**Snow.** If snow settles on the unit, brush it gently off the top and away from the sides with a soft broom. Don't use hot water.

**Don't box it in.** A tight box or a tarp over the unit while it's running blocks airflow and traps defrost water. Our [heat pump covers](/covers/) page explains what kind of cover is safe.

## Heat pump winter faults worth catching early

These winter faults are worth a call before they get worse:

- Warm air that is only lukewarm at the indoor unit
- The outdoor unit running constantly but the room never getting warm
- Ice that doesn't clear
- Steam or water coming from inside the house, not outside
- A new grinding or rattling noise on start-up

Some are a clean, and some need a [repair](/heat-pump-repair/). An older unit that struggles every winter may be a candidate for a [cold climate heat pump](/cold-climate-heat-pumps/) built for low outdoor temperatures.
