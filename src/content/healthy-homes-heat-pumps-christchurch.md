## The Healthy Homes heating standard, in plain terms

If you rent out a home in Christchurch, the main living room needs a fixed heater that can heat it. The rule is specific:

- **One or more fixed heaters** that can directly heat the main living room to at least 18°C.
- **Sized to the required heating capacity** for that room.
- **A heat pump used for it must have a thermostat.**
- **Open fires and unflued combustion heaters don't qualify.**

That's the test. A plug-in heater doesn't count because it isn't fixed. An open fire doesn't count whatever it does to the room. A heat pump does count, as long as it's the right size and it has a thermostat.

## Working out the healthy homes heat pump capacity your rental needs

The capacity isn't a guess. It's a figure worked out for the main living room, and it depends on the room itself:

- **Floor area and ceiling height.** A bigger room, or one with a high ceiling, needs more heat.
- **Insulation in the ceiling, walls and underfloor.** The less there is, the more capacity the room needs.
- **Windows.** How much glass, whether it's single or double glazed, and how much sun the room gets.
- **The floor.** A room over a cold, uninsulated floor loses more heat than one over a warm space.
- **Where the home is.** Christchurch's climate is part of the calculation.

You can work the figure out with the Tenancy Services [heating assessment tool](https://tools.tenancy.govt.nz/heating-tool), or with the formula set out in the regulations. We do it for the room we're heating before we recommend a unit, so the heat pump we fit matches the number.

## The 2.4 kW line and newer homes

Two details catch landlords out.

**Over 2.4 kW, a heat pump is the electric option that counts.** If the main living room needs more than 2.4 kW, a fixed electric heater other than a heat pump can't be used to meet the standard. Whatever you fit, each qualifying heater has to be at least 1.5 kW. We check the unit's rated heating output against the room's figure, not just the model name.

**Newer homes use a different formula.** Homes built to the 2008 building code are assessed with a formula that usually gives a smaller capacity figure. That's because they're better insulated and hold their heat. The upshot is often a smaller unit than an older villa the same size would need. We check which formula applies to your rental before we size anything.

Landlords need a heat pump that meets the standard and paperwork to prove it. That's what we give you.

- **The right size, worked out properly.** We measure the main living room and work the capacity figure for it, then pick a unit that meets it.
- **The model and capacity in writing.** You get them for your records, so you can keep them with your tenancy file and show them if you're asked.

## Choosing a landlord heat pump for a rental

For most rentals, a [high wall heat pump](/high-wall-heat-pumps/) in the living room is the simplest fit. It's out of the way, it's what tenants expect, and it meets the thermostat requirement. A [floor console](/floor-mounted-heat-pumps/) suits a room with no high wall space, or one where you want the heat down low.

What matters more than the model is the size. A unit that's too small won't heat the room to 18°C on a cold morning and won't meet the capacity figure. A unit that's too big cycles on and off and costs more to run. We size to the figure and pick from the brands we fit.

If the living room is open to the kitchen and dining, we size for the whole open space. If it's a separate room with a door, we size for the room. The standard is about the main living room, so that's where the heat pump goes.
