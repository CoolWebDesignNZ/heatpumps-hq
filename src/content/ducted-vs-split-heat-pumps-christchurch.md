## How a ducted or split system works

A **split system** has an indoor unit in the room, piped to an outdoor unit. The indoor unit is usually a high wall unit or a floor console. One split heats one room or one open space. You can have several round the house.

A **ducted system** has one indoor unit hidden in the roof space or under the floor. It pushes warm air through insulated ducts to a vent in each room. It pulls air back through a return grille. As [Daikin New Zealand](https://www.daikin.co.nz/pages/ducted-heat-pumps) puts it, once it's in, you only see the controller and the grilles.

In between sits the **multi split**. That's one outdoor unit running two or more indoor units in different rooms.

## Ducted vs split system side by side

The biggest difference is reach. A split heats the room it's in. Ducted heats every room with a vent.

| | Split system | Ducted system |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rooms heated | The room it's in | Every room with an outlet |
| What you see | A unit on the wall or floor | Ceiling or floor grilles and a controller |
| Control | Each unit set on its own | One controller, with zones if fitted |
| Space needed | An outside wall and a spot for the outdoor unit | Roof or underfloor space for the unit and ducts |
| Install work | At the wall and outside, one room at a time | A larger job through ceilings and the roof space |
| Best timing | Any time | New builds and renovations that open ceilings |
| Noise on the label | Indoor and outdoor figures, where shown | Outdoor figure only, where shown |
| If it fails | Other rooms keep their heat | The whole system is affected |

## When a split system makes more sense

Splits are the usual pick in these cases.

- **You mostly want the living area warm.** One well sized unit in the lounge may be all the house needs. Maybe a second for the main bedroom.
- **The roof space is tight.** A low pitch, a skillion roof or a ceiling full of services can make ducting hard or costly.
- **The house is older and finished.** In a villa, cutting vents and running ducts through lath and plaster ceilings is a big job. A wall unit is far simpler.
- **You want to spread the cost.** Start with the room that matters most. Add another later.
- **You want each room on its own.** If one unit has a fault, the rest keep going.

The trade-off? You see the units on the walls. Each system has its own outdoor unit. And rooms without a unit rely on warm air drifting in.

## When ducted makes more sense

Think hard about ducted in these cases.

- **You want the whole house warm.** Bedrooms, hall and bathroom too, not one warm room and cold corridors.
- **You don't want units on the walls.** Ducted leaves walls and floors clear. Nothing to work the furniture round.
- **It's a new build or a big renovation.** With the ceilings open, ducts go in with far less mess.
- **There's good roof space.** A pitched roof with room for the unit and clear routes to each room.
- **You'll use zoning.** A zone controller lets you heat the living areas by day and the bedrooms at night. See [heat pump zoning](/heat-pump-zoning/).

The trade-offs? It's a bigger job up front. You need roof or floor space. And one system serves every room, so a fault hits the whole house.

## Comfort and airflow from ducted vs high wall heat pumps

It comes down to where the warm air comes into the room. A high wall unit blows it across the room from one spot. A floor console lets it out low down. Put either in the wrong place and a big or L-shaped room can end up uneven.

Ducted vents spread the air from several spots, usually in the ceiling. Warm air rises. So where the vents go, and how hard the air blows, decides whether the heat gets down to where you sit. A good ducted design places the vents and the return air path so each room gets an even share.

## Running costs of ducted vs split heat pumps

You can only compare them like for like. Heating one lounge with a wall unit uses less power than heating the whole house with ducted. Whole house against whole house is closer. Four or five wall units against one ducted system depends on the models. It depends on how efficient they are when it's cold, and how you run them. Check the energy labels. EECA's [label guidance](https://www.eeca.govt.nz/assets/EECA-Resources/Product-regulations/The-new-Zoned-Energy-Rating-Label-for-heat-pumps-air-conditioners.pdf) explains the kWh figures and star ratings for the Cold zone that applies to New Zealand.

Zoning is one of the main ways to keep ducted running costs down. It stops you heating empty rooms.

## Noise from ducted and split systems

With a split, the indoor fan is in the room. Modern units are quiet on low fan. But you'll hear them at full output on a cold morning. With ducted, the indoor unit is in the roof space. What you hear is air moving at the vents.

Outside, both have a compressor and a fan. Whole-house ducted outdoor units are often bigger. EECA's label guidance notes that the outdoor noise figure matters if the unit's near a window, a bedroom or a neighbour. [Christchurch noise limits at the boundary](/heat-pump-boundary-rules-consent-nz/) apply to both types.

## Servicing ducted and split systems

With splits, you clean the filters in each room. Each unit needs a regular service. Ducted usually has a filter at or near the return air grille. Clean it often. The unit in the roof needs access for servicing. Either way, a yearly [service](/maintenance-servicing/) keeps it efficient.

## Ducted or split system? A quick way to decide

Start with how many rooms you want warm, and what the house allows.

- One or two rooms, older house, tight roof: **split system**.
- Three or four rooms, not much roof space: **multi split** or several splits.
- Whole house, new build or good roof space: **ducted**.
